\begin{abstract}
A scene graph is normally a record: a data structure that perception fills and planning reads. We
propose the \emph{Active Scene Graph} (\asg), a scene graph that acts to keep itself true. Every node
is a concept with a posterior and a precision; every concept publishes what the robot could do to
reduce its own uncertainty; the robot's body is one such concept and calibrates itself the same way;
and a single controller ranks every offer in one currency, \nats, and executes them in real time over
a distributed graph. We state this as five requirements over standard scene graphs: actionable,
epistemically self-constructing, concept-oriented, embedding a self-calibrating robot model, and
real-time over a distributed architecture. Each requirement has prior art; the claim of this paper is
that they are \emph{coupled}, so a system that meets them must meet them together, and that we have
one. We give the model (concept nodes with posterior, precision and existence; affordances as priced
offers; one expected free energy with a task prior as the only difference between representing and
acting), the architecture that meets the concurrency requirements (a CRDT graph, a zero-copy media
plane, and two protocols verified in TLA+), and an evaluation of the parts that run, in simulation
and on two physical robots. We report the results that support the requirements and the ones that do
not: on \num{594} annotated floor plans the epistemic viewpoint selector is statistically
indistinguishable from a coverage heuristic, and we explain why the endpoint could not have
discriminated. A ledger separates what is designed, implemented and evaluated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Three-dimensional scene graphs have become the standard representation for robots that must reason
about places, objects and their relations~\cite{armeni20193dscenegraphstructure,rosinol20203ddynamicscenegraphs,hughes2022hydra,gu2023conceptgraphsopenvocabulary3dscene}.
In every one of these systems the graph is a \emph{record}. Perception writes nodes and edges;
planning reads them; the graph itself neither knows how uncertain it is nor does anything about it.
When the record is wrong, nothing inside the representation notices, and when it is incomplete,
completing it is somebody else's job: an exploration module driven by a frontier, a task planner
driven by a language model, or a person with a joystick.

This paper describes a scene graph that is not a record. In an \emph{Active Scene Graph} the
representation is the thing that acts. Every node is a \emph{concept}: a belief with a mean, a
covariance, a precision and a log-odds of existence, maintained by an agent that owns it. Every
concept publishes, as nodes in the same graph, the actions the robot could take to reduce that
concept's uncertainty, priced in \nats. The robot's own body is a concept in the same sense, whose
kinematic and extrinsic parameters are beliefs corrected online by the same machinery that corrects
everything else. A controller ranks every published offer, whatever its source, under one expected
free energy, and a task is nothing more than a prior preference added to that functional. The
representational layer and the task layer differ by one term.

We state the idea as five requirements (\S\ref{sec:req}). Each has prior art, and we make no claim to
be first on any of them. Our claim is different and, we argue, stronger: the requirements are
\emph{coupled}. An actionable offer needs a calibrated probability behind it; a calibrated
probability needs a solved self-model; several writers need ownership; ownership needs a transport
that guarantees cleanup; bounded bandwidth needs a graph that carries beliefs rather than pixels;
and the self-model learns only because acting is the experiment. Satisfying the requirements one at
a time does not compose. Every one of these dependencies was found the hard way, as a measured
incident in a running system, and we report the incidents.

The contributions are: (i) the five requirements and the coupling argument (\S\ref{sec:req});
(ii) the model: concept nodes with posterior, precision and existence, affordances as priced offers,
and one expected free energy for both regimes (\S\ref{sec:def}, \S\ref{sec:efe}); (iii) the robot as
a concept that calibrates itself from the corrections that ordinary actions generate
(\S\ref{sec:self}); (iv) an architecture that meets the concurrency requirements, with two
protocols verified in TLA+ (\S\ref{sec:arch}); (v) an evaluation of the running parts in simulation
and on two physical robots, including a null result and its cause (\S\ref{sec:eval}); and (vi) an
honest ledger of what is designed, implemented and evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}).

This preprint extends our IWAI abstract~\cite{bustos2026active}, which stated the actionable-graph
idea for an active-inference audience with a Fisher-information viewpoint selector, and the
concept-first architecture of~\cite{zapatacornejoConceptGuidedExplorationBuilding2025}, which built
rooms and doors as agents but with intentions rather than a formalisation: no stated joint, no
priced offers, no lifecycle contract, no self-model. What is new here relative to both is the
requirement set, the coupling argument, the single-currency pricing, the self-calibrating robot
concept, and the evaluation.

\subsection{The coupling argument}\label{sec:coupling}

A checklist can be satisfied one row at a time. The requirements above cannot, and the reason is a
set of dependencies between them, each of which we met as an incident in a running fleet. We state
them as arrows (Fig.~\ref{fig:coupling}).

\paragraph{R1 needs R2 needs R4} An offer's price is a log-probability. The precondition of a
pragmatic offer (is the door open?) is only meaningful if the posterior behind it is calibrated, and
that posterior was only calibrated once the self-model's extrinsics were solved: a boresight error
of $0.8^\circ$ in one camera put every corner it saw a few centimetres off, in a way that averaged
into a smaller declared uncertainty rather than a larger one. Actionable implies calibrated implies
a self-model.

\paragraph{R2 needs R3} Epistemic self-construction with several writers requires ownership. Without
it, two agents held different values of ``the target'' and no term in either state could reveal the
disagreement; the return channel between them was a boolean. The cure was an edge the consumer owns
plus an epoch, which is a statement about ownership, not about inference.

\paragraph{R3 needs R5} Ownership is only enforceable if the transport guarantees that a graceful
exit runs cleanup and that a transient peer flap does not delete a live node. A one-second flap at
startup made an agent tear down its own nodes and exit cleanly; the fix was a debounced presence
protocol, which is a transport property.

\paragraph{R5 needs R2} Bandwidth is bounded only because the graph carries beliefs, a mean and a
covariance per concept, rather than sensor data. The media plane exists so that the graph does not
have to.

\paragraph{R4 needs R1} Acting is the calibration experiment. There is deliberately no ``calibrate''
affordance; the self-model learns from the corrections that ordinary offers generate. A robot that
never acts never calibrates.

\section{Related work, by requirement}\label{sec:related}

We organise prior work by which requirements it meets rather than by system, and summarise in
Table~\ref{tab:related}.

\paragraph{Scene graphs as records} The 3D Scene Graph~\cite{armeni20193dscenegraphstructure}
introduced the layered structure (building, rooms, objects, cameras); Kimera and its dynamic scene
graphs~\cite{rosinol20203ddynamicscenegraphs,rosinol2021kimeraslamspatialperception} and
Hydra~\cite{hughes2022hydra,hughes2024foundations} built it online from a metric-semantic
reconstruction; ConceptGraphs~\cite{gu2023conceptgraphsopenvocabulary3dscene} and
HOV-SG~\cite{werby24hovsg} made the labels open-vocabulary; Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronosunifiedapproachspatiotemporal}
added time. All are single-pipeline systems in which the graph is an output. None exposes the
uncertainty of a node as something the robot acts to reduce, and none carries actions in the graph.

\paragraph{Rooms and walls in the loop} S-Graphs~\cite{bavle2022situational,bavleSGraphs20HierarchicalSemantic2025}
is the closest work on R2 and R3 for the room layer: walls and rooms are factors in one optimisation,
so the structure constrains the pose. It is still a single estimator and the graph is still passive.

\paragraph{Task-driven graphs} Clio~\cite{maggioClioRealtimeTaskDriven2024} clusters the graph to
the task at hand; SayPlan~\cite{ranaSayPlanGroundingLarge2023} plans over a scene graph with a
language model. In both the task lives outside the representation and the graph is what the task
reads. In an \asg\ the task is a prior added to the same functional the graph already minimises.

\paragraph{Active SLAM and active inference in robotics} The active-SLAM
survey~\cite{placedSurveyActiveSimultaneous2023} frames exploration as decision-making under
uncertainty and makes the point we lean on in \S\ref{sec:eval}: an open-loop dataset cannot score a
closed-loop policy. Active inference~\cite{friston2010free,friston2015epistemic} supplies the one
functional we use; its robotics applications~\cite{lanillosActiveInferenceRobotics2021,pezzatoMobileManipulationActive2025}
have concentrated on control and manipulation rather than on the representation.

\paragraph{Our own prior work} CORTEX~\cite{bustos_cortex_2019,proposal-latency-bustos} is the
distributed working-memory substrate we inherit. The concept-first architecture
of~\cite{zapatacornejoConceptGuidedExplorationBuilding2025} showed rooms and doors as agents; the
IWAI abstract~\cite{bustos2026active} stated the actionable graph with a Fisher-information
selector. Neither gave the requirements, the coupling, the pricing, or the self-model.

\paragraph{The term} ``Active scene graph'' is used in computer vision for active learning of
image scene-graph generators. \todo{search and cite one representative; disambiguate in a footnote}.
We use the word in the control sense: a graph that acts.

\section{Definition: the Active Scene Graph}\label{sec:def}

\subsection{Nodes are concepts}

A node $n$ is a tuple $(c,\,\mu_n,\,\Sigma_n,\,\Pi_n,\,\ell_n,\,a_n)$: a class $c$, a posterior mean
and covariance over the class's parameters, a precision $\Pi_n$ attached to the observation channel
that feeds it, a log-odds of existence $\ell_n$, and the identity $a_n$ of the agent that owns it.
The parameters are class-specific: a room is a polygon of corners with a pose; a table is a box with
a height; a bottle is a cylinder; a door is a hinge line, a width and an opening angle. Each class
comes with a generative model of what the sensors return given the parameters, written as a signed
distance field so that the likelihood is differentiable in the parameters: the room's polygon SDF
against LiDAR returns, a table as a smooth log-sum-exp box top with four cylindrical legs, a bottle
as a cylinder. The same SDF serves for the fit and, projected through the camera model, for the
expected information gain of a candidate view.

\subsection{Edges are typed and timestamped}

Geometry is carried by \texttt{RT} edges holding a rigid transform, a timestamp and a covariance, so
that a consumer can extrapolate a lagging frame with the producer's twist and can weight a
transform by how much it is trusted. Three further edge types carry the active part:
\texttt{has\_intention} from a concept node to the affordance nodes it publishes; \texttt{executing}
from the controller's own node to the affordance it is currently executing; and \texttt{proto}, a
self-edge marking a node whose identity is not yet resolved (a room the robot has just entered
through a door and has not yet recognised as new or as one it has been in before).

\subsection{Agents are owners}

Every node is created, updated and removed by exactly one agent, and the agent's identity is stored
on the node. The agent is the process that runs the class's generative model. It is a peer in a
distributed graph, not a plugin in a pipeline: it starts, declares which peers it needs, serves
while they are present, and cleans up its own nodes when it stops.

\subsection{The lifecycle contract is the operational semantics}

What an agent may do to the shared graph is fixed by a contract with five stages.
\emph{Create}: a node is born only from a tracker that has associated several frames, never from a
single detection. \emph{Update}: a frame may move geometry only if its frame is resolvable, its
subject is centred in the image and the robot is still enough that the interaction-matrix variance
does not dominate. \emph{Remove}: the only removal evidence is ``predicted visible but absent'',
weighted by \pdet\ and requiring line of sight through the walls; occlusion holds the belief and a
miss counter is forbidden. \emph{History}: no agent may use a constant process noise; the
volatility of a concept is inferred from its own history. \emph{Ownership}: the identity stored on
the node must match the agent that writes it. A conformance audit (\S\ref{sec:eval:r3}) checks every
agent against every stage.

\section{One objective, two regimes}\label{sec:efe}

Every quantity the controller compares is a log-probability. There is one functional, the expected
free energy of a policy $\pi$,
\begin{equation}
\efe(\pi) = \underbrace{-\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log C(o_\pi)\big]}_{\text{pragmatic}}
\;-\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[H(s) - H(s\,|\,o_\pi)\big]}_{\text{epistemic}},
\label{eq:efe}
\end{equation}
and the task layer differs from the representational layer only by the prior preference $C$.

With a flat $C$ only the epistemic term survives. This is the fleet's default: move so as to
represent, and stay aligned with the world. It is the bottom, always-on layer, and it is what an
\asg\ does when nobody has asked it for anything. With a sharp $C$ over a goal state (``the robot is
in room B'') the pragmatic term dominates, and an epistemic offer off the route is taken exactly when
its information gain exceeds the pragmatic loss of the detour, in the same units. ``Take the detour
if it does not perturb the goal too much'' is this inequality, not a rule.

Two properties make the table work across a fleet. Each producer computes $\Delta H$ from its own

executor never learns what a door is: the affordance's kind is a label used for logging, never a
dispatch key, so a new concept adds offers without touching the controller.

\subsection{Interruption is re-scoring}

Selection, horizon and interruption are one comparison. The incumbent, if any, is the offer under an
\texttt{executing} edge; the challenger is the minimiser of $\efe$ over everything offered; a switch
fires when the incumbent's price exceeds the challenger's by the commitment margin. Producers decay
their epistemic gain as their $\Sigma$ shrinks during execution, so an incumbent that is nearly
resolved loses to a fresh challenger without a rule saying so; the interruption emerges from the
value dynamics. The margin is the one constant left in this part of the system, and
Table~\ref{tab:price} says so.

\section{The robot as a concept}\label{sec:self}

The robot is a node in the graph with a frame tree beneath it: base, sensor mounts, and the rigid
transforms between them. What distinguishes it from a table is only that its parameters are also the
parameters of the measurement model of everything else. R4 says these parameters are beliefs, and
that they are updated online.

\paragraph{What is learnable} On the platforms we use, low-level velocity loops hide the dynamics, so
there is only a kinematic model to learn: wheel-odometry scale and rotation gain, and the extrinsics
of each camera relative to the LiDAR. Dead reckoning cannot calibrate itself, because an odometry
error and a true motion are indistinguishable from inside the odometry. What breaks the symmetry is an
absolute reference, and the localiser already has one: the room polygon, against which the SDF
anchors the posterior. Its error does not grow with distance travelled, so it is an independent
witness of true motion.

\paragraph{The free teacher} The localiser's own correction is the teacher. Every cycle it predicts
the pose from the motion model and corrects it from the map; the correction is the residual the
calibration must explain. A batch estimator over episodes solves jointly for the kinematic and
extrinsic parameters that minimise the corrections, under the same free energy the localiser
minimises over states. The parameters are separable only if each loads on a different component of
the correction times a different covariate, which is why rotation gain (loads on heading, covariate
angular speed) and translational scale (loads on position, covariate linear speed) can be told apart,
and why the mount height of a camera cannot be separated from the height of the ceiling it sees
(\S\ref{sec:eval:r4}).

\paragraph{Why there is no calibration affordance} It would be natural to publish ``calibrate
yourself'' as an offer. We do not, and the reason is the arrow R4$\to$R1 in Fig.~\ref{fig:coupling}:
the free energy of the self-model does not fall because the robot computed harder, it falls because
the robot moved and the map disagreed. We measured this directly: across an injected 0 to 10\% scale
error the solver's iteration count was flat at 13.3 to 13.9, while the correction load
$|\text{estimated} - \text{predicted}|$ tracked the error. Acting is the experiment; the ordinary
offers generate all the evidence the self-model needs.

\paragraph{Downward coupling} A correction to a parameter propagates into every belief that was fitted
through it: the corrected extrinsics re-price every offer that depends on where a camera points. The
rule is that this propagation may only loosen a belief, never tighten it; slower convergence is the
correct behaviour. This is specified and not built (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}).

\section{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

R3 and R5 are met by an architecture with four parts.

\paragraph{The graph} A CRDT graph replicated on every agent's machine, inherited from
CORTEX~\cite{bustos_cortex_2019,proposal-latency-bustos}. Each agent holds a full copy; writes are
merged without a coordinator; signals fire on every peer when a node or edge changes. Every attribute
is typed at compile time.

\paragraph{The media plane} Sensor streams do not enter the graph. A producer advertises, on the
sensor's node, a descriptor naming a topic on a dedicated DDS domain; consumers subscribe to it
zero-copy. The graph carries beliefs and the descriptor; the plane carries pixels and points. This is
the R5$\to$R2 arrow: bandwidth is bounded because the representation is parametric.

\paragraph{The presence protocol} An agent declares the peers it requires, starts serving only when
they are present, and degrades when one leaves. Degradation is debounced: a transient flap does not
trigger cleanup. The protocol was modelled in TLA+ and checked exhaustively: eight safety invariants
and four liveness properties over \num{65536} states for a three-agent instance. The model abstracts
heartbeat timing away, and the failure that later stopped the fleet was a timing failure (the
undebounced flap). A proof about a specification and a specification that survives contact are
different achievements, and we report both.

\paragraph{The affordance protocol} A producer publishes an offer with a target and an epoch; the
controller claims it by writing an \texttt{executing} edge it owns, carrying the epoch and the pose
it is actually driving to. The producer measures progress against the consumer's stated target,
never its own latest proposal, and a mismatch of epochs is detectable by both sides. The earlier
version of this protocol returned a boolean, and the TLA+ model of it reproduces the failure in a
four-state counterexample; with the claim carried, the model is clean at three times the bound

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Premise}

A dataset comparison does not apply to this work, and we say so rather than supply one. A dataset is
an open-loop recording; under \eqref{eq:efe} the observations received depend on the actions chosen,
so replaying a fixed sequence scores one policy on data another policy generated. The comparison that
is meaningful is policy against policy inside one closed loop, with body, sensors, noise, timing and
environment held fixed and only the decision rule changed~\cite{placedSurveyActiveSimultaneous2023}.
Our comparators are the heuristics each mechanism replaced: each was written by people who wanted it
to work, tuned until it did, and shipped, so it cannot be called a weakened baseline.

Platforms: a Webots simulation of a two-room apartment running the same component code as the
robots; a differential-drive robot (Shadow) with a 360$^\circ$ LiDAR, a stereo camera and a
panoramic camera; and a second differential-drive robot (P3Bot) with a different LiDAR and no
panoramic camera. The fleet is the same on all three; the robot concept differs.

\subsection{R2: the uncertainty is consumed}\label{sec:eval:r2}

\paragraph{Calibrated corners on a physical robot} The room concept declares a corner publishable when
its posterior $\sigma$ falls under a bound. On the physical robot, against a surveyed room, a corner
declared publishable has a median true error of \SI{2.0}{cm} and lies within its declared
\SI{6}{cm} in \SI{86}{\percent} of cases; a withheld corner has \SI{3.6}{cm} and \SI{64}{\percent}.
The gate is doing what the requirement says: the declared uncertainty separates good corners from
bad ones. The full study is in a companion paper; we do not repeat its figures.

\paragraph{Room shape from an epistemic explorer} On \num{50} synthetic rooms with adversarial spurs
and alcoves, replacing a coverage heuristic by the information-gain explorer raised the median
layout IoU from \num{0.947} to \num{0.954} and the worst room (an L shape) from \num{0.812} to
\num{0.944}.

\paragraph{The null, and why} On \num{594} real annotated floor plans, paired by room, the same
explorer against the same heuristic gives a mean IoU difference of $-0.0007$ with a 95\% confidence
interval of $[-0.0019,\,+0.0004]$; the heuristic wins 263 rooms, the explorer 251, with 69 ties. The
epistemic term buys nothing on this corpus. The cause is the endpoint: every room in both arms ran to
the 900-frame budget (594 of 594 and 593 of 594), so the final map was measured at saturation, where
any reasonable policy has already seen every wall. An objective cannot be graded on an endpoint its
own budget has saturated. The number that predicts the null is the 594/594 at the cap, and it was
available before the run. What is supported is that the uncertainty is consumed: it gates publication
and the explorer reads it. What is not supported is that the epistemic term buys accuracy or speed
over a simple heuristic at single-room scale. Tighter budgets and multi-room environments are where a
difference would have to appear, and they are untested.

\subsection{R1: one executor, offers from any concept}\label{sec:eval:r1}

The affordance claim protocol is evaluated formally (\S\ref{sec:arch}) and live: after the epoch and
the owned edge were introduced, the refusal loop in which a re-armed offer looked new to the
controller every cycle fell from \SI{47}{\percent} of decisions to \SI{7}{\percent}, and the
protocol-event flood the producers generated to reconcile their view of the target
(\num{12} per second) went to zero. The door offers (approach, open, cross) are built and compile
against the same executor with no door-specific code in the controller; they have not yet been run
live, and Table~\ref{tab:ledger} says so. The task-level experiment the outline calls for, one room,
one door, one live epistemic offer, with the controller logging the decomposition of \efe\ per
candidate per decision, is the first item of future work.

\subsection{R3: the lifecycle contract has teeth}\label{sec:eval:r3}

A conformance audit checks each of eight concept agents against the five stages of the contract and
three sub-conditions (admissibility of a frame, \pdet-weighted absence, line of sight through walls).
At the audit of 2026-07-31, five agents conformed on every column, two conformed with an attributable
history missing, and one (the human concept, inherited) failed every stage but ownership; it is
scheduled for rewrite rather than reported as conforming. The clearest effect of the contract is on
phantoms: bringing the bottle concept under the create stage (birth from a tracker, never a single
frame) reduced phantom births on a fixed tour from \num{130} to \num{1}. Ownership is enforced at
startup: an agent refuses to start if the node it would own carries another agent's identity.

\subsection{R4: the self-model learns from its own corrections}\label{sec:eval:r4}

\paragraph{Extrinsic attribution} On one recorded tour we inject a $1^\circ$ yaw error into the
panoramic camera, into the stereo camera, and into the LiDAR in turn, and read what the
closed-loop extrinsic estimator recovers. The stereo camera's yaw is recovered at
$0.984 \pm 0.018$ of the injected value, the panoramic camera's at $0.454 \pm 0.175$, and an error
injected into the LiDAR shows up in both cameras and not in their closure ($-0.018$), which says
which device is misaligned. The asymmetry between the cameras is itself a result: on a panorama a
yaw is a constant pixel shift, indistinguishable from a corner's own offset, while on a pinhole the
shift grows towards the field edge as $\sec^2\theta$, so one corner seen across the image separates
yaw from offset by itself. The panorama's uniformity, which is what lets it separate pitch from
height, is exactly what costs it yaw. The pair is complementary.

\paragraph{A bias the precision could not see} The estimator found a boresight error of
$+0.814^\circ$ in the stereo camera that the corner channel had been absorbing as a smaller
declared $\sigma$ rather than a larger error. Applying it is what let the corner calibration above
hold. This is the R1$\to$R2$\to$R4 chain measured.

\paragraph{What must not be published} The same estimator produces a mount height and a pitch for
each camera. We do not publish them. Floor corners and ceiling corners disagree by \SI{116}{mm}
about the height of one mount, while agreeing on its yaw to $0.0006^\circ$; the height channel is
measuring the ceiling constant of the room, not the mount. A parameter is only learnable if it loads
on a covariate nothing else loads on, and here it does not.

\paragraph{Odometry} Wheel-odometry covariance is injected per cycle as a function of speed, and the
rotational variance had to be raised by a factor of \num{580} from the vendor's constant before the
localiser's normalised innovation was consistent. A zero-velocity update whose $\sigma$ was measured
on one robot proved 5.9 to 7.3 times too tight on the other, which is the reason the robot concept,
not a shared constant, owns it.

\subsection{R5: latency and membership}\label{sec:eval:r5}

\SI{27}{ms} (RGB-D) old when the simulator hands it over, and the plane adds under
\SI{10}{ms} above that. The LiDAR stream runs at \SI{20}{Hz}, its source rate.

\subsection{Localisation and object beliefs against ground truth}

Two numbers from the same fleet, reported so that the beliefs the graph carries can be judged as
beliefs. Against simulator ground truth over 4 runs and \num{5468} poses, the room-anchored
localiser has a median position error of \SI{72}{mm}, \SI{145}{mm} at the 90th percentile, and
$0.8^\circ$ in heading, at \SI{19.1}{Hz}. For the bottle concept, against ground truth over
\num{2173} rows, the position RMSE is \SI{13.6}{mm} with a median normalised estimation error
squared of \num{0.46}: the covariances are honest, which is what a belief-based graph must show
before its offers can be priced.

\section{Limitations and claim discipline}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item The epistemic term is not shown to beat a heuristic at single-room scale, and we have
explained why the endpoint could not show it. The multi-room, budget-limited experiment is not done.
\item The task-level functional (a sharp $C$) is designed and not implemented; the R1 evaluation is of
the protocol, not of a task.
\item Two constants remain in the controller: the commitment margin, and $\lambda$ until it is
replaced by the calibrated $Q$. Both are named in Table~\ref{tab:price}.
\item The CRDT history is unbounded: the per-node version vector grows with every write, and on long
sessions the merge cost was measured at one full core on \num{318000} entries. Either it is bounded or
the claim is scoped to session length; today it is the latter.
\item The detectability envelope \pdet\ is fitted in simulation for the classes reported here; its
calibration on real images is reserved for a separate paper and not claimed.
\item The self-model's translational scale inherits the map's error: a room polygon 2\% too large
hands 2\% to the scale, confidently. Rotation is immune; translation is only as good as the survey.
\item Doors: the proto-room mechanism that lets the robot navigate while a new room is being
estimated is built and compiled but has not been run live.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

An Active Scene Graph is a scene graph whose nodes are beliefs, whose beliefs publish what would
reduce them, whose robot is one of its own beliefs, and whose controller prices everything in one
currency. We have argued that the five requirements this entails are coupled, shown a system that
meets them together, and reported what its evaluation supports and what it does not. The next
version of this preprint adds the task-layer experiment on the physical robot and the multi-room
setting in which an epistemic explorer could be expected to matter.
