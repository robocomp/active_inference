Self-Model Coupling Atlas

A robot that infers only where it is has learned half of what it needs. The other half is what it is — the scale of its own wheels, the tilt of its own cameras. Both are factors in one generative model, and both are identified the same way: by the covariate that moves them. This page draws the two blocks as Jacobians, and shows which parameters they cannot tell apart.

base block · 253 episodes / 203 m optical block · 395 171 pairs / 23 corners every correlation below is measured, not schematic

One model, two blocks

Each block is a least-squares residual whose Jacobian columns are the parameters and whose rows are the components of a disagreement. Two parameters are coupled when they load on the same row, and degenerate when their covariates on that row are proportional. So the whole question of what a robot can learn about itself reduces to one thing: which covariates does its own motion supply, and in what mixture.

Block I

The base against the room

An episode is a stretch of driving between two corrections from the localiser. The residual is what the wheel odometry failed to predict, in the robot's own frame, and it has three components.

The one parameter the robot cannot learn

k_lat comes back at 0.998 of its prior — the data moved it by two parts in a thousand. Its only covariate is lateral travel, and a differential drive has none.

This is not a defect and no manoeuvre repairs it. It is the estimator correctly reporting that a question was never asked, and it is the shape every genuinely unidentifiable parameter takes: a posterior indistinguishable from its prior.

Block II

The cameras against the LiDAR

A room corner is one physical edge seen twice: the LiDAR finds the wall–wall intersection at sensor height, the camera finds the floor–wall–wall triple point. Push the LiDAR's corner through the camera's mount alone and the localiser's pose never enters the expression — so this block measures a hand–eye disagreement, not a pose error.

Why the yaw row carries two sigmas

The solve reported ±0.0017° from 395 171 pairs. Those pairs are 23 corners sampled thousands of times each, and the corners disagree with one another by 5.3 px while the mount signal is 1.1 px.

Counting them as independent understates the uncertainty by 127×, against a design effect √(395 319 / 23) = 131 — the two agree, which names the mechanism rather than merely flagging a gap. The fitted yaw is then smaller than its own error. Giving δv a slot in the model and integrating it out does not recover that precision. It reveals we never had it.

Block II · extended

Recovering a wrong extrinsic, and naming the device

★ Two different relations live on one corner, and they do not measure the same thing. Everything above is the camera against the LiDAR. What follows adds the camera against the camera, states the algebra that decides whether a deliberately wrong mount can be recovered at all, and reports the test — run 2026-09-03 on one tour: ricoh 91 152 pairs over 21 corners, zed 11 821 over 20, twenty corners seen by both.

The two relations, and what each one is blind to

Complementary, not redundant. The pair gives each mount but cannot exclude a shared fault; the closure excludes the shared fault but cannot attribute it. Three devices are what turn disagreement into attribution — and that had never been tested against a fault whose location was known in advance.

Marginalising the corner's own offset · the Schur complement

ri = −Ji θ + δv(i) + εi δv ~ N(0, S), S = σδ² I σδ = 5.3 px, measured per corner: Av = Σ JᵀWJ cv = Σ JᵀW Dv = Σ W bv = Σ JᵀWr ev = Σ Wr Mv = ( S⁻¹ + Dv )⁻¹ H̃ = Σv ( Av − cv Mv cvᵀ ) b̃ = Σv ( bv − cv Mv ev )

A covariance term, not a rejection rule: σδ → 0 recovers the old estimator exactly, σδ → ∞ removes the level entirely. The limit is the point — as a corner is seen more often Mv → Dv⁻¹ and it contributes only its within-corner information, so the effective sample size becomes the number of distinct corners, not the number of sightings.

What decides whether a wrong mount comes back

inject θ₀ into the mount ⇒ the prediction moves by Jθ₀ ⇒ r → r − Jθ₀ ⇒ b → b − Hθ₀ Δθ̂ = ( H + I )⁻¹ H θ₀ per eigendirection: λ / (1 + λ) recovery = 1 − ( σpost / σprior )² the fraction of prior variance the data removed
consequence 1
Recoverability is a property of the information, not the amount of data. A corner seen again from the same place raises no λ and buys no recovery.
consequence 2
The injection experiment is predictable from the posterior alone. Measured: the identity predicts 0.454 for the ricoh against 0.454 recovered, and 0.992 for the zed against 0.984 — so injecting is a consistency check on the estimator, not a second independent fact.

Why a panorama loses its yaw and a pinhole does not

equirect: u = (α/2π + ½)·W ∂u/∂δ = W / 2π CONSTANT across the image pinhole: u = fx·x/y + cx, θ = atan(x/y) ∂u/∂δ = fx · sec²θ GROWS toward the edge of the field

A constant shift is exactly the signature of δv. So on the panorama yaw and a corner's own offset are degenerate and only the spread across distinct corners separates them; on the pinhole one corner seen at several places in the image separates them by itself. ★★★ The panorama's virtue is what costs it yaw — the uniform angular resolution chosen to break the pitch/height ridge is the same property that creates the yaw degeneracy.

A prediction that failed, and was worth more than one that held

The zed's recovery was predicted at 0.477 by the design-effect argument — the same reasoning that gave 0.463 for the ricoh, against 0.454 measured. The zed returned 0.984.

Not a small miss: the wrong model. The design effect assumes the within-corner information about yaw is nil, which is exactly true of a panorama and false of a pinhole. What replaced it is a sharper claim than the one that failed — recoverability is set by the projection's geometry, is readable in advance from σpost/σprior, and differs per camera in a way that makes the pair worth more than either alone.

Where the two blocks touch

The blocks are estimated separately, but they are not independent. Two couplings cross between them, and each one means a number from one block cannot be read without a number from the other.

① the camera's time offset is not a time cam_dt = T · k_v → a dt in pixels is a delay only once the base's own scale is known, and only from the SAME run. Read alone, it is a velocity in disguise. ② the mount's yaw and the corner's own offset arrive identically ∂r_u/∂yaw = ∂r_u/∂δ_v = constant → within one corner they cannot be told apart at all. Across corners, only the SPREAD of δ separates them — 23 numbers, not 395 171.
The excitation is the same question in both blocks

Every parameter above is identified by one covariate, so the informative motion is whatever makes that covariate move. Driving toward a corner varies range and feeds pitch and height. Driving past it sweeps bearing and separates a metric map error from a pixel detector bias. Turning feeds k_ω; distance feeds k_v and δk_wheel.

Only one parameter wants something the work does not already supply. b_ω's covariate is elapsed time alone, so its information-maximising action is the one that spends time and nothing else — the optimal excitation for a gyro bias is to stand still. A zero-velocity update, arrived at by derivation rather than adopted as a technique.

An apartment tour supplies every other covariate for free: a turn greater than half a radian every 3.6 m, 46 rad per 100 m. The need for a calibration manoeuvre and its cost are the same variable, so an affordance must overcome its own justification — and the only one this platform justifies is a pause.

What the robot learns, and what changes when it does

The parameters are not the deliverable. A running robot never reads them; it drives, predicts, and is corrected. So the question that matters is where each learned number enters that loop, and which measurable thing moves when it does. Both answers turned out to be different from the ones assumed at the start, and the difference is the most useful thing on this page.

The loop the module lives in

Nothing here is a calibration procedure. The localiser is already computing, every cycle, the exact quantity the parameters are fitted to — how far its own correction had to move the prediction. Ordinary driving is the experiment, and the corrector is the teacher.

The result to state, and the one to stop stating

Calibration reliably reduces the error the optimiser must remove, and reliably does not reduce how often or how hard it solves.

A healthy robot's accuracy does not improve, and that is the correct outcome rather than a disappointing one: the localiser was already removing the odometry's scale error either way. Grading calibration on corrected output measures the corrector. The endpoint that answers is the optimiser's input — the correction load — because nothing downstream can absorb it and, unlike a firing count, it cannot saturate.

The effort reading did not survive repetition. A 4.4× drop in firing appeared once and came back 5.41 → 5.08% under the same conditions; firing sorts by session, not by treatment, and between-session drift exceeds every within-session effect. Iterations per solve — the actual work — never moved at all. Free energy is not compute: at 0.5 m/s and 10 Hz a 10% scale error makes ~5 mm of drift per cycle, and Gauss–Newton converging from 2.2 mm instead of 1.5 mm takes the same 13 iterations.

★ The two non-significant results above look alike and are not, and their remedies are opposites. Firing is a bad endpoint: it counts whether an indicator crossed a threshold, so it discards magnitude and saturates, and no amount of driving repairs it — replace it. Healthy-robot accuracy is a sound instrument on too small an arm: ~500 m per leg would give it 80% power — drive further. Discounting an effect for missing significance in an underpowered arm is the exact mirror of believing one because it is large.

What survives is sharper than what was claimed: the correction load is the prediction error the self-model failed to account for. Reducing it is the whole of what self-calibration does for a running robot — and on a broken one, that is the difference between 72.6 and 36.0 mm/m as k_v converges, i.e. a 10%-mis-scaled robot recovering to as-new accuracy while still driving.

How the knowledge is held between runs

What persists on disk is evidence — the information matrix and its right-hand side — never fitted values. Restoring (H, b) and re-adding the prior at solve time resumes a measurement; restoring a fitted number would be a ratchet that no later observation could argue with. The same rule sets the shape of a future update: a parameter learned downstream may only loosen the belief it came from, never tighten it.

And there is no calibration behaviour to schedule. Every covariate above is supplied by an apartment tour for free — a turn over half a radian every 3.6 m — so the need for a manoeuvre and its cost are the same variable, and an affordance would have to overcome its own justification. The single exception is b_ω, whose only covariate is elapsed time: the optimal excitation for a gyro bias is to stand still.

What it will not learn, however long it drives

Four limits are structural, and each shows up as a posterior that will not leave its prior: k_lat (no lateral travel to ask with), dt (no edge into any residual), a detector bias common to every corner (it is yaw, and nothing internal separates them), and the absolute mounts (a rotation shared by all three devices is invisible to a test that compares them — closure shows the devices tell one story, never that the story is true).

A fifth is quantitative and sharper. With the per-corner offset in the model, the data's information about camera yaw falls to the between-corner scale — ±0.216° of data against a ±0.201° prior. A 1.0° yaw error is therefore predicted to come back as ≈0.46°, not 1.0° predicted · not yet run: from a wrong start, on 23 corners, a camera's yaw cannot be fully calibrated away by driving.

The remedy that follows is not more driving past the same corners — tracking adds rows to a cluster, never new clusters — but more distinct corners. That is a statement about where to take the robot, which is the most actionable thing the whole exercise produced.
