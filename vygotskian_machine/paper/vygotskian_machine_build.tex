\begin{abstract}
We extend \cortex{}, an existing architecture in which Active Inference agents maintain a probabilistic hierarchy of
beliefs about the robot, its rooms, the objects in them and aggregates of objects, and in which a language agent
already plans and talks with a person, with a pretrained vision-language model (VLM) that stays frozen. Because the
robot cannot change the model, the knowledge it carries remains outside the robot and becomes the robot's own only
through its experience, which is the course Vygotsky described for higher mental functions, first between people and
then within the individual. We use his account to organise the design, not as a theory to be modelled. We add two things. The
first is an inner voice, a loop in which the VLM proposes questions and tests and only the
robot's perception changes the answers, called on by a demand signal built from the surprise that the agents report.
The second is a cultural plane, in which the VLM supplies a level-4 hypothesis about what each room is for, writes
missions whose steps predict their own belief outcomes, and imagines on the robot's own models while demand is low.
The VLM's knowledge enters as pseudo-counts and stored missions that the robot's own experience progressively
replaces. We relate the proposal to Sutton's bitter lesson and to the argument that intelligence cannot be fully
amortised, and we design seven experiments, not yet run. The Vygotskian claim rests on whether a VLM stripped of
the culture attached to its words falls behind with novelty, and a further test asks whether the robot's reuse of
plans is selected by its own uncertainty rather than by matching past requests.
\end{abstract}
